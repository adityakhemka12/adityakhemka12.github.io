  <section class="research-paper">
    <h3>The Inequality Multiplier: Market Inelasticity and the Persistence of Wealth Inequality</h3>
    <p class="paper-authors">
      <a href="https://chrishyland.github.io/">Charles Christopher Hyland</a>
      and Aditya Khemka
    </p>
    <p><span class="paper-status">Submitted</span></p>

    <p>
      We study how frictions in financial intermediation transmit income
      inequality into wealth inequality. When mandate-constrained
      intermediaries maintain stable allocations between equity and debt,
      household saving and borrowing flows affect equity prices rather than
      being absorbed entirely through quantities. Because equity ownership
      is concentrated, these revaluations redistribute wealth toward the top.
      We call this amplification mechanism the inequality multiplier. We
      develop a general equilibrium model with heterogeneous households and
      calibrate it to the United States from 1989 to 2023. Inelastic pricing
      roughly doubles the response of wealth inequality relative to an elastic
      market benchmark.
    </p>

    <p>
      <strong>Award:</strong> Saïd Foundation Award for Best Doctoral Paper in Finance, 2025
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Presentations:</strong> EEA-ESEM 2026 Dublin (August 2026);
      ISB Summer Research Conference (July 2026); AFA Annual Meeting Poster
      Session (January 2026); Bank of England Macro Brown Bag (July 2025);
      Asian Finance Association, Taipei (June 2025); Trans-Atlantic Doctoral
      Conference, London Business School (June 2025); Rethinking Economic
      Theory Conference, Athens (December 2024)
    </p>

  <section class="research-paper">
    <h3>Green Policy, Unequal Outcomes</h3>
    <p class="paper-authors">
      Aditya Khemka and
      <a href="https://tsomocos.org/">Dimitrios P. Tsomocos</a>
    </p>

    <p>
      We investigate how climate-focused financial regulations affect wealth
      inequality and economic welfare. We develop a two-period general
      equilibrium model with heterogeneous households, incomplete markets,
      and endogenous climate damages to assess green capital requirements
      and carbon taxes. Green capital regulations reduce pollution, but can
      constrain credit allocation, lower aggregate efficiency, and amplify
      wealth inequality. Carbon taxes reduce emissions and cause a smaller
      rise in inequality.
    </p>

    <p>
      <strong>Presentations:</strong> Bank of England Financial Stability
      Seminars (September 2025); Bank of England PPD Research Discussion
      Group (May 2025); Oxford Saïd–VU SBE Macro-finance Conference
      (June 2024); IV Central Bank Conference on Environmental Risks,
      Mexico City (December 2023)
    </p>

      <a href="https://ssrn.com/abstract=6303041">SSRN</a>

  <section class="research-paper">
    <h3>
      Climate Change, Macroprudential Policy Responses and Their
      Distributional Consequences in South Africa
    </h3>
    <p class="paper-authors">
      Aditya Khemka,
      <a href="https://profiles.open.ac.uk/christina-laskaridis">Christina Laskaridis</a>,
      and
      <a href="https://tsomocos.org/">Dimitrios P. Tsomocos</a>
    </p>
    <p class="paper-venue">South African Reserve Bank Working Paper Series, 2025</p>

    <p>
      We study the environmental, macro-financial, and distributional risks
      South Africa faces in transitioning from coal-dependent growth to a
      low-carbon economy. Using a two-period computable general equilibrium
      model calibrated to South African data, we compare carbon taxes, brown
      risk-weighted capital surcharges, and green capital discounts. Carbon
      taxation most sharply curbs emissions and, when revenues are rebated to
      workers, also narrows wealth and consumption inequality.
    </p>

    <p>
      <strong>Presentation:</strong> Committee of Central Bank Governors in
      SADC Climate Change Policy Dialogue, Lusaka (July 2025)
